Radon is not a smoke-alarm problem. You cannot see or smell it, and a phone notification cannot remove it. A continuous monitor can be useful because it shows how readings move over hours and days, but the useful output is a **decision trail**: where the device sat, what changed in the building, whether the reading was confirmed, and what a qualified professional measured after mitigation. The [EPA radon program](https://www.epa.gov/radon) says testing is the only way to know a home's radon level; the monitor is one way to observe, not a reason to skip a valid test.

## Start with the decision, not the gadget

Before buying or moving a device, write down the decision you need to make. A homeowner checking an untested basement, a renter documenting a concern, a buyer in a real-estate transaction, and a household verifying a mitigation system have different evidence needs. Transaction rules, state programs, device instructions, and professional standards can differ. A consumer monitor that is convenient for trend watching may not satisfy a transaction or legal requirement.

Use this four-question intake:

| Question | Why it changes the plan | Evidence to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Has the home ever been tested? | Establishes whether this is screening or follow-up | Prior report, date, floor and test method |
| Is a sale, lease or renovation pending? | Deadlines and accepted methods may differ | Contract, state guidance, renovation scope |
| Is there already a mitigation system? | Verification must include system operation and retesting | Installer report, fan status, post-work test |
| Which rooms are occupied? | Exposure decisions depend on actual use, not the easiest outlet | Lowest occupied level and room use |

The [CDC testing guide](https://www.cdc.gov/radon/testing/index.html) distinguishes short-term tests, which run from 2 to 90 days, from long-term tests lasting more than 90 days. Longer observation better reflects a year-round average, while a short-term test can support a faster screening decision. A continuous monitor may make variability visible, but its display does not rewrite those categories.

## Choose a representative location

Placement errors can make a precise-looking number misleading. Follow the exact device or kit instructions. In general, use the lowest level that is regularly occupied or could be used as living space, not a crawlspace chosen only because it seems close to the soil. Keep the device away from exterior doors, open windows, strong fans, direct heat, obvious drafts, high humidity, and places where children or pets can move it.

Do not hide it in a cabinet or put it directly on the floor unless the manufacturer instructs that. Record the room, height, distance from openings, start time, and any closed-house conditions required by the test. Take one wide placement photo without exposing family documents, network credentials, serial numbers, or the device account screen.

![A clean lowest-level room prepared for a representative radon test](/images/illustrations/hms-radon-monitor-20260825-01.png)

## Read the time series without cherry-picking

Radon can change with weather, pressure, ventilation, foundation conditions, and building operation. That makes hourly data informative but easy to misuse. One high hour is not a diagnosis; one low afternoon is not a clearance. Keep the raw timeline and compare consistent windows rather than selecting the most reassuring screenshot.

Use three views:

1. **Immediate anomaly view:** Did placement, power, ventilation, or device movement change?
2. **Short screening view:** What does the instructed multi-day period show under valid conditions?
3. **Long-term view:** Does a 90-day-or-longer observation indicate a persistent seasonal pattern?

A simple weighted dashboard should not average invalid periods with valid ones. Mark device setup, open-house activity, major storms, HVAC changes, construction, or long power outages. Do not alter normal living conditions merely to force a lower number. If the device lost power, record the gap rather than interpolating a convenient value.

| Pattern | Plausible interpretation | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| Brief spike after device movement | Placement artifact is possible | Restore valid placement and restart as instructed |
| Repeated elevated multi-day periods | Persistent entry or pressure pattern is possible | Arrange confirmation under accepted protocol |
| Large difference between floors | Location and foundation pathways matter | Test representative occupied areas |
| Low result immediately after mitigation | Encouraging, not final proof | Complete the specified post-mitigation test |

## Use a confirmation ladder

Do not jump from a consumer notification to an expensive contractor quote, and do not dismiss an elevated trend because the app later fell. The safe middle is a confirmation ladder.

**Step 1: validate the setup.** Check model instructions, calibration or quality-control requirements, location, test duration, power continuity, and environmental limits. Do not open, modify, or “calibrate” a sealed device using an online trick.

**Step 2: preserve the original result.** Export the report if possible, but also retain the device name, serial reference, test dates, units, location, and screenshots that do not expose account data.

**Step 3: contact the state radon program.** EPA's [radon zone map and state information](https://www.epa.gov/radon/epa-map-radon-zones-and-supplemental-information) is useful for resources and contacts, but a zone map cannot determine one home's result. Ask which test methods and professional credentials apply locally.

**Step 4: run the appropriate confirmation.** Follow EPA/state instructions and transaction requirements. If buying or selling, EPA and CDC recommend qualified measurement help rather than improvising a casual second placement.

## Translate the result into a household decision

EPA's action level is commonly expressed as 4 pCi/L, and CDC directs households at or above that level toward a licensed professional for reduction. Treat the threshold as an action point, not a claim that lower exposure is harmless. The [CDC health overview](https://www.cdc.gov/radon/about/index.html) explains that radon can cause lung cancer and that smoking plus radon increases risk.

A household decision table should separate health precautions, building work, and evidence:

| Confirmed situation | Household step | Building step | Evidence step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Result below action level but home was never tested long term | Maintain normal use; avoid false reassurance | No unrequested construction | Consider long-term testing |
| Elevated screening result | Avoid panic and do not tamper with the building | Seek qualified assessment | Confirm by accepted method |
| Elevated result during a sale | Follow contract and state process | Do not conceal or alter conditions | Use qualified measurement records |
| Post-mitigation result | Keep system powered and unobstructed | Follow installer maintenance | Retest on the specified schedule |

This article cannot estimate personal cancer risk or declare a home safe. Discuss health concerns with a clinician and building decisions with the state program and qualified radon professionals.

## Compare mitigation proposals by scope, not price alone

Radon reduction is not simply “install a fan.” A qualified professional should investigate foundation type, entry routes, pressure field, occupied spaces, electrical work, discharge location, drainage conflicts, and local code. EPA's [consumer reduction guide](https://www.epa.gov/radon/consumers-guide-radon-reduction-how-fix-your-home) describes common approaches and the need for testing after work.

Ask every bidder for the same written fields:

- measurement evidence and the protocol used;
- proposed technique and foundation areas served;
- fan, piping, sealing, electrical, condensate, and discharge scope;
- permits or inspections where required;
- expected diagnostic and post-mitigation tests;
- warranty, noise, energy use, labels, and maintenance;
- who is credentialed and who performs each task;
- exclusions, change-order triggers, and total installed cost.

Use a weighted comparison, but keep hard gates. Example: protocol and credentials 30%, design completeness 25%, verification 20%, warranty/service 15%, price 10%. A low price cannot compensate for missing post-work testing or an unsafe discharge plan. EPA's [professional locator](https://www.epa.gov/radon/find-radon-test-kit-or-measurement-and-mitigation-professional) and state program should be checked before signing.

## Build a realistic cost and runtime estimate

Separate one-time installation from ongoing ownership. Create rows for diagnostic testing, permit, core mitigation, electrical work, sealing, post-test, fan electricity, future fan replacement, periodic retesting, and repairs after renovation. Use written quotes and local utility rates; do not copy a national average into a household budget.

For annual electricity, use:

`fan watts ÷ 1,000 × 24 hours × 365 × utility rate`

A hypothetical 70-watt fan at $0.18/kWh would use 613.2 kWh and cost about $110 per year. This is an illustration, not a product estimate. The actual fan may operate differently, and safety, diagnostics, and verified reduction matter more than optimizing a few watts.

Add outage planning. A radon fan or monitor may stop during a blackout. Do not connect equipment to an improvised power source or extension arrangement. Follow manufacturer and installer instructions, record the outage, and confirm system status after power returns. The site's [smart-home surge and outage inventory](/posts/smart-home-surge-protector-ups-outage-recovery-inventory-2026/) can help organize power dependencies without pretending a UPS is a mitigation design.

## Renter and multi-unit boundaries

A renter should not drill, seal structural gaps, or install ventilation equipment without authorization. Preserve the measurement method and notify the landlord or property manager through the documented maintenance channel. Ask which party will arrange qualified testing, what access is required, and how results will be shared. Local tenant, housing, and disclosure rules vary; state radon and housing agencies are the appropriate source.

In a multi-unit building, one unit's result may not characterize every unit. CDC notes that the lowest level within the unit is the relevant starting point. Do not place a device in a common mechanical room without permission or represent a hallway result as a unit result.

## Verify the system after work

A mitigation project is incomplete until the result is tested. Confirm the indicator is in its normal state, vents are unobstructed, covers and seals are intact, drains still function, and the discharge location remains compliant. Do not open a sealed sump cover or touch electrical components to inspect them.

Retest after major renovations because changes to foundations, HVAC, windows, insulation, or pressure can change radon. Keep the original report, proposal, permit, photos, post-test, warranty, and maintenance instructions together. A smart-home backup plan can preserve documentation, but the [home backup guide](/posts/home-assistant-backup-plan-2026/) should not be used to automate safety-critical control without professional design.

## Final checklist

- Define whether the decision is screening, transaction, health follow-up, or post-mitigation verification.
- Follow the exact placement and duration instructions.
- Preserve raw readings, location, conditions, and gaps without cherry-picking.
- Confirm elevated trends through the state/EPA-accepted process.
- Use EPA/state resources to verify qualified measurement and mitigation professionals.
- Compare proposals on protocol, design, verification, warranty, and total ownership cost.
- Do not use an air purifier, app automation, or a single low reading as a radon solution.
- Retest after mitigation and significant building changes.

A good smart monitor makes uncertainty visible. A good radon plan turns that uncertainty into valid testing, qualified work, and verified reduction. The technology is valuable when it supports that chain—and dangerous when its clean dashboard replaces it.
